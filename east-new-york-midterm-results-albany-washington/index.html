East New York's Midterm Results: From Albany to Washington

A look at who East New York voters chose in Albany and Washington and what the preliminary results mean for residents.

By Deric JohnsonEast New York Times7 min read

East New York's election picture includes a commanding lead for Assemblymember Nikki Lucas in unofficial Board of Elections returns, an open congressional seat won by Claire Valdez and another term for Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, according to race calls. Final November vote totals remain subject to the completed count and certification.

The neighborhood's representation stretches across several districts. In Albany, the local picture includes Christian Celeste Tate, Latrice Walker, Lucas, Julia Salazar and Roxanne Persaud. Their districts have separate boundaries, so a resident's representatives depend on their address.

The results determine who is positioned to represent residents in the next legislative terms. Uncontested candidates, projected winners and a candidate leading an unfinished count are not the same category.

Assembly District 54: Christian Celeste Tate

Christian Celeste Tate was listed as unopposed in the November general election for Assembly District 54, which includes parts of East New York and Cypress Hills. An uncontested race is distinct from a certified final result.

Tate reached the general election after defeating incumbent Erik Martin Dilan in the June Democratic primary. The NYC Board of Elections' certified primary record shows Tate with 4,192 votes and Dilan with 2,550. Those figures establish the primary outcome, not the November general-election totals.

His primary victory set the stage for a change in the district's representation. The completed November canvass will establish the official general-election count.

Assembly District 55: Latrice Walker

Assemblymember Latrice Walker ran unopposed in District 55, and the Associated Press results listing identifies her as the winner. That election call is separate from certification of the final vote totals.

Walker chairs the Assembly's Election Law Committee, according to her official biography. Her role places her work at the center of state legislation governing elections.

District 55 adds another Assembly office to the local picture, alongside Districts 54 and 60. Residents should check their address against current district boundaries rather than assume that everyone in the same neighborhood shares an Assembly member.

Assembly District 60: Nikki Lucas holds a commanding lead

Assemblymember Nikki Lucas held a commanding lead over challenger Norman H. Ramsay in unofficial election-night returns from the Board of Elections in the City of New York.

Lucas had 22,349 votes, or 93.53%. Ramsay had 1,510 votes, or 6.32%, combining 1,307 votes on the Republican line and 203 on the Conservative line. There were 37 write-in votes, for 23,896 votes counted.

The board's returns, last updated at 12:41 a.m. on Nov. 4, showed 96.51% of scanners reported. The results remain unofficial until the canvass is complete and the count is certified.

Lucas first won the seat in a February 2022 special election. District 60 includes parts of East New York, Brownsville and Canarsie. If her lead holds through the completed count, she will continue representing the district in Albany.

Her current committee assignments include Housing, Health, Aging, Labor, Energy, and Economic Development, Job Creation, Commerce and Industry. Those assignments connect her work to housing, health care, employment and household expenses.

Senate District 18: Julia Salazar

State Sen. Julia Salazar represents District 18, which includes Cypress Hills and portions of East New York, along with other Brooklyn and Queens communities. She ran unopposed in the November election, according to the election listing reviewed for this report.

Salazar chairs the Senate's Committee on Crime Victims, Crime and Correction. Her office serves residents within District 18, separate from those represented by District 19.

Senate District 19: Roxanne Persaud

State Sen. Roxanne Persaud also ran unopposed. District 19 includes East New York, Cypress Hills, Brownsville, Starrett City and other southeastern Brooklyn communities.

Persaud chairs the Senate's Social Services Committee. Her legislative role provides another point of contact for residents following state programs and services affecting their households.

The presence of both Salazar and Persaud in the neighborhood's representation shows why an address-level district check matters. East New York does not have a single state senator serving every resident.

Congressional District 7: Claire Valdez wins an open seat

Claire Valdez, a sitting member of the state Assembly, won the open 7th Congressional District seat, according to an Associated Press race call. She is the representative-elect until the next congressional term begins.

Valdez will succeed retiring Rep. Nydia Velázquez in the 7th Congressional District, while Hakeem Jeffries will continue representing the neighboring 8th District. Both races have been called, though final vote totals remain subject to certification.

Congressional District 8: Hakeem Jeffries wins reelection

The Associated Press called the 8th Congressional District race for Democratic incumbent Hakeem Jeffries over Republican Lewis Mizrahi. That is a projected outcome, not a certified final count.

Jeffries' office submits community project funding requests and provides information about federal grant opportunities. A submitted request does not guarantee an award.

His housing record offers one example of how federal funding can reach East New York. His office reports securing $3 million in 2022 for Habitat for Humanity's Project Constellation, an initiative covering eight sites across East New York and several other Brooklyn neighborhoods. The planned 71 affordable homes were spread across the participating neighborhoods, not all located in East New York.

That distinction matters when assessing an announcement. A project that includes the neighborhood is not necessarily located entirely within it.

What residents can track after the election

The results identify who is positioned to hold office. Public records can help establish what their work delivers.

At a state housing budget hearing, Lucas asked how many homes proposed under New York City's housing goals would be located in East New York, particularly Assembly District 60. She also asked how aspiring homeowners could benefit.

At a separate health budget hearing, she requested district-level or ZIP-code-level information about the health care workforce and participation in workforce programs. She questioned whether the programs were reaching her district equitably.

Those questions provide concrete subjects for follow-up: where homes will become available, who qualifies, how many residents enter training programs and how many obtain jobs. They are part of Lucas's existing legislative record.

The same standard applies across the local delegation. Announcements can be checked against funding records, eligibility rules, implementation schedules and completed work.

Final totals remain pending

The New York State Board of Elections identifies election-night returns as unofficial. Officials must complete the canvass, including valid ballots not included in the initial returns, before certification. Totals and percentages can change during that process.

The state election calendar sets Nov. 28 as the deadline for county boards to certify and transmit general-election returns. Dec. 15 is the last day for the State Board of Canvassers to meet to certify the general election. Those are deadlines, not guaranteed release dates for an individual race.

For East New York, the next stage is both electoral and practical: confirming the final outcomes, then tracking legislation, access to programs, funding awards and completed neighborhood projects.
