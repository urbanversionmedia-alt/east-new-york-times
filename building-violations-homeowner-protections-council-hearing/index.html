Can a Building Violation Put Your Home at Risk? Council Takes Up Homeowner Protections Tuesday

A joint Council hearing will weigh bills to warn owners before unpaid Buildings Department fines turn into tax liens, enroll homeowners in property tax payment plans automatically and study who gets hit hardest by violations.

By Deric JohnsonThe East New York TimesNovember 16, 20264 min read

For many East New York homeowners, a Department of Buildings violation can look like a fine to deal with later. Under city law, it can become something more serious.

When an owner of a one-, two- or three-family home loses a Buildings Department case and leaves the penalty unpaid, the judgment can become a tax lien on the property. That lien carries interest at the same rate as unpaid property taxes, and the city can sell it or foreclose on it the same way it does unpaid taxes, according to Section 28-204.6 of the city's construction code.

In fiscal year 2026, the Department of Finance turned 892 Buildings Department violations, worth a combined $6.6 million, into property liens, according to the department's annual ECB report, released this month.

On Tuesday, Nov. 17, the Council's Committee on Housing and Buildings and Committee on Finance will hold a joint oversight hearing, “Advancing Homeownership Opportunity and Protection,” at 11 a.m. at 250 Broadway, 8th Floor, Hearing Room 2. Fourteen items are on the agenda.

The bills to watch

Warning on the violation itself (Intro 1091). Sponsored by Council Member Mercedes Narcisse, this bill would require Buildings Department violations that can become liens to say so on the notice. Within three days of a judgment, the department would have to tell the owner about payment plans, deadlines and ways to avoid or clear the lien. The Buildings and Finance departments would also publish yearly data on lien conversions and do outreach to owners of one- and two-family homes (Intro 1091).

“My bill ensures homeowners receive proactive notice when building violations could lead to a tax lien, equipping them to address issues early,” Narcisse said in a Sept. 25 Council announcement introducing the package.

Automatic property tax payment plans (Intro 1084). Council Member Crystal Hudson's bill would have the Finance commissioner automatically enroll eligible homeowners in the city payment plan with the lowest total cost. Owners would get notice at least 60 days before the first payment and could opt out (Intro 1084).

Who gets cited, and where (Intro 1097). Deputy Speaker Nantasha Williams' bill would require the Buildings Department to study racial, geographic and income gaps in how violations are enforced and how cases turn out, then report to the mayor and Council speaker (agenda).

Outreach where displacement is highest (Intro 1096). Also from Williams, this bill would direct the city's Homeowner Advocate to work with the Mayor's Office of Deed Theft Prevention and other agencies on ongoing outreach to homeowners in the community districts with the highest displacement rates (agenda).

“Homeownership remains one of the most important ways families build stability and generational wealth, but protecting that investment has become increasingly complicated for too many New Yorkers,” Williams said in the same announcement.

The agenda also includes bills on first-time homebuyer aid through HPD's HomeFirst program and on helping Section 8 voucher holders buy homes.

How to weigh in

Residents can testify in person without registering in advance, according to the Council's testimony page. Written testimony is accepted up to 72 hours after the hearing ends. Questions about the hearing can go to hearings@council.nyc.gov.

Homeowners who already have a converted Buildings Department lien can pay it online at nyc.gov/ecbpropertyliens, according to the Department of Finance.

The East New York Times will cover Tuesday's hearing.
