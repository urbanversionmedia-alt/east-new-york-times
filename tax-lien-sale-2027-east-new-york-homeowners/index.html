The Tax Lien Sale Is Coming Back. Here's What East New York Homeowners Should Know Now.

City officials told the Council the sale will return in 2027 with new protections. But East New York homeowners still pay about twice Park Slope's effective property tax rate, and the city has no reform bill yet.

By Deric JohnsonThe East New York TimesNovember 17, 20267 min read

The city plans to bring back the tax lien sale in 2027, and officials say homeowners will have more ways to stay out of it this time.

But at a City Council hearing Tuesday, council members said a bigger problem for neighborhoods like East New York is still not fixed. According to the mayor's own housing plan, homeowners in East New York, Canarsie and Cambria Heights pay effective property tax rates about twice what homeowners in Park Slope and the East Village pay.

The hearing was a joint session of the Committee on Housing and Buildings and the Committee on Finance.

Housing Chair Pierina Sanchez said the city has historically used the lien sale to recover unpaid property taxes, water and sewer bills and certain repair charges. The administration suspended the sale in 2026 to review the program, she said, but it has since been reported that the city will hold a lien sale in mid-2027.

"As you can imagine, there are lots of my colleagues here for whom that is very alarming," Sanchez said.

Sanchez said it was the Council that is allowing the administration to pursue a lien sale in 2027, and that the Council put "many reforms" in place. She credited Council Member Sandy Nurse, whose district includes parts of East New York, and the Abolish the Tax Lien Sale Coalition for their work and recommendations on those reforms.

No date yet

Department of Finance officials did not give a date. Asked how many properties the city expects to come off the list before the sale, a DOF official said, "At this point we're just in the planning stages. We don't have any set data or set timeline."

Sanchez said that in past sales, about 85 percent of properties came off the list before the sale took place.

What's changing

Jeffrey Shear, DOF's first deputy commissioner, and Deputy Commissioner Annette Hill laid out the changes planned for 2027:

A higher foreclosure threshold. For owner-occupied Tax Class 1 homes, the foreclosure minimum will rise to $70,000 or more than 15 percent of the property's market value. Shear said the change addresses concerns about homeowners losing their homes over relatively small amounts.

A higher bar for water debt. For two- and three-family homes, water and sewer debt will need to reach $5,000, up from $3,000, and be more than a year old before it puts a home in the at-risk pool. Single-family homes remain exempt.

Time for families after a death. Properties with deceased owners will come off the sale for two years so families can resolve ownership.

Earlier warnings. The city will share the at-risk list with nonprofit partners before the 90-day notice period begins, and agencies will share more data so more homeowners get text and email alerts.

Scam warnings. Lien sale notices will include information about deed theft, scams and predatory practices.

More ways to pay. The city is expanding water debt assistance, offering low-income customers the same kind of payment agreement with the Department of Environmental Protection that DOF offers, and opening its Easy Exit option to more complex ownership setups such as LLCs and trusts.

DOF also said it is streamlining referrals to free legal help for homeowners who need it.

Who will knock on doors

HPD Commissioner Levy said her agency holds a $2 million, one-year outreach contract with the Center for New York City Neighborhoods, now called the Center for New York Home Ownership. She said the Center is bringing on between 15 and 20 community-based groups to reach homeowners on the list, address by address.

Levy said the Center has committed to eight touch points with each homeowner, including mailings, texts, phone calls, door knocking and in-person counseling.

Council Member Joseph said older adults are targeted for scams and may not trust people who say they are from the city. Levy said that is why the Center will work with community groups "who might be more recognizable to seniors."

DOF said it will hold two outreach sessions in each of the 10 Council districts with the most properties in the at-risk pool. Officials did not say which districts those are. During the hearing, the districts of Council Members Selvena Brooks-Powers and Banks, whose district includes parts of East New York, were named among the communities most at risk.

Brooks-Powers said tax liens sold on small homes in her Southeast Queens district went from 44 in 2021 to 102 in 2025, and that foreclosures there are the highest in the city. She asked DOF to commit extra outreach money to her district. A DOF official said the agency would reach out to all districts.

The tax gap

The new rules do not change how much homeowners are taxed in the first place.

Council Member Mercedes Narcisse cited the mayor's housing plan, which says homeowners in Canarsie, East New York and Cambria Heights pay effective property tax rates about twice what Park Slope and East Village homeowners pay. She asked what DOF would change and on what timeline.

A DOF official said the agency is working with City Hall on property tax reform and hopes to have legislation for the upcoming state legislative session. Pressed later by Deputy Speaker Nantasha Williams, DOF said, "We do not yet have a bill. We are working on developing a bill."

Williams said the state has repeatedly said it is waiting for the city to send something up.

"I do not believe that you all really care, and I do not believe you all are actually actively working towards property tax reform," she said.

What the last sale showed

DOF officials said 3,144 Class 1 owners came off the most recent lien sale list by enrolling in a payment plan. Asked how many homeowners lost their homes through tax lien foreclosures in the past year, DOF said, "We had none."

Later in the hearing, Bradley McCormick, an attorney with Brooklyn Legal Services who represents deed theft victims, said there have been tax lien foreclosure auctions in 2026. He described one homeowner who owed $20,000 in tax arrears on a property DOF valued at $1.5 million. The property sold at a foreclosure auction for $1 million, he said.

DOF also said 3,859 properties that receive the senior or disability homeowner exemption have outstanding property taxes. The agency said it is producing a report on the size and age of those debts and whether those owners are in payment plans, and expects to have it within the next couple of weeks.

What homeowners can do now

You do not have to wait for the 2027 list to come out. Officials at the hearing pointed to these options:

Get on a payment plan. DOF offers six different property tax payment plans, including PT AID and reduced-interest plans for owners who qualify. Officials said enrolling in a payment plan removes a property from the lien sale list.

Get free help. HPD's Homeowner Help Desk connects homeowners to housing counseling and legal services, including help for owners at risk of foreclosure. Call HPD at 212-863-6300 and ask for an HPD navigator.

Watch out for deed theft. The city's Office of Deed Theft Prevention has received about 300 complaints since it opened in March, officials said.

Williams said the Council's homeownership expo is coming to Brooklyn in January. A date and location have not been announced.

The East New York Times will update this story as city agencies release East New York figures on the 2027 sale.
