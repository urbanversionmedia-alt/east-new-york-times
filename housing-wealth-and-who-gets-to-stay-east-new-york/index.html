Housing, Wealth, and Who Gets to Stay in East New York

At CB5’s final meeting of the season, Sandy Nurse pressed for housing vouchers, NYCHA repairs, and homeownership pathways while East New York’s next Assembly member, Christian Celeste Tate, watched from the audience.

Council Member Sandy Nurse speaks at Community Board 5’s final general meeting of the season in East New York. Photo: Urban Version Media / East New York Times.

By Urban Version MediaEast New York Times6 min read

As Community Board 5 wrapped its last meeting of the season, the question hanging over the room was simple and urgent: who actually gets to stay in East New York as the city spends billions on housing and services, and who gets pushed out of the picture. The political stakes were clear from the moment Council Member Sandy Nurse stepped to the podium, and newly elected Assembly Member-elect Christian Celeste Tate took a seat in the audience after what Nurse called a “very hard-fought election” the day before.

When Nurse was introduced, the room erupted into prolonged applause that clearly signaled how closely this community is watching her housing and budget fights. She opened by acknowledging the work of the outgoing Assembly member who has represented the area for years—but notably did not mention Erik Dilan by name—before turning to the future. She thanked the board and residents for the warm welcome and publicly recognized Tate, congratulating him on his win and saying she looks forward to working with him, while he remained in the audience and did not speak.

Nurse used her remarks to frame the city budget as a referendum on shelter versus permanent housing. She said she is not prepared to vote for a budget that doesn’t expand CityFHEPS vouchers and move the city away from what she described as an “expensive, bloated shelter system” and toward long-term apartments for low-income families. She explained that when the Council passed voucher expansion last year, it was designed to move about 30,000 to 40,000 families out of shelter and into housing and also help with skyrocketing utility costs, but the administration refused to implement the law and instead sued the Council.

East New York’s housing fight is not just about what gets built next. It is about whether the people already here can stay, buy, repair, and work in the neighborhood they helped hold together.

For East New York, the stakes are obvious. Local NYCHA developments still have long waiting lists while many units sit empty because they need lead and asbestos remediation. Nurse said NYCHA currently rehabilitates about 300-500 vacant units per month citywide and argued they should be pushed to 1,000 units per month to match the scale of need. She urged board members and residents to “help hold the line” by contacting the administration and demanding it drop the lawsuit and sit down to negotiate a real expansion of vouchers and repairs.

On the ground, that decision will help determine whether families now living in shelter can move into permanent housing nearby, or whether they will be relocated farther from their schools, churches, and support networks.

At the state level, Assembly Member Nikki Lucas focused on a different side of the housing crisis: homeowners who are one bad contract or one confusing notice away from losing their houses. Her office reported that she pushed to increase statewide deed-theft enforcement funds to $4.5 million, split between the Attorney General and District Attorneys, and secured $40 million for the Homeowner Protection Program, which pays for legal services, advocacy, and education. Lucas’ New Lots Avenue office now functions as a legal triage center, with a rotation of free attorneys handling deed theft, foreclosure threats, landlord-tenant issues, and even tax preparation.

In East New York, where investors and speculators have aggressively targeted long-time owners, those resources are a direct line of defense for elders and working-class homeowners who are vulnerable to pressure and misleading paperwork. Once a deed is gone, so is a generation of stability on that block, which is why Lucas is emphasizing early intervention and walk-in access to attorneys close to home.

Nurse also reminded CB5 that she and colleagues in the Progressive Caucus fought to revive “Open Doors,” a city homeownership program where HPD builds houses or apartments for low- and moderate-income buyers, and said the administration has now doubled its funding. On paper, that sounds like the kind of ownership pathway East New York residents have been asking for. In practice, it raises hard questions: how many of those units will actually be built in CB5, at what income levels, and with what outreach to current residents who want to buy without leaving the neighborhood.

Who Is Christian Celeste Tate?

  • Christian Celeste Tate, the newly elected Assembly Member-elect, was in the audience at CB5’s final general meeting of the season after what Council Member Sandy Nurse described as a “very hard-fought election yesterday.” Nurse thanked the outgoing Assembly member for many years of service—without using Erik Dilan’s name—and then turned her attention to Tate, saying she is looking forward to working with him to tackle issues in the district and offering her congratulations from the podium.
  • Tate did not address the board, choosing instead to listen as Nurse outlined her budget priorities on housing and vouchers and as Assembly Member Nikki Lucas’ office detailed new state-level funding for homeowners and workforce programs. His quiet presence, formally acknowledged from the front of the room but not yet tested by questions from the floor, underscored how much is in motion: a sitting Council Member tightening her housing line, a sitting Assembly Member directing new resources into East New York, and a newly elected Assembly Member preparing to step into that landscape.

Jobs are part of that equation. CB5’s Second Vice Chair Charles Bullock promoted the Carpenters Union’s Building Works pre-apprenticeship program, which offers a two- to three-week boot camp leading directly into the union. Lucas’ office added that they are pairing OSHA classes with real job placements and said about 40 residents from one site have already been placed into work, with added mental-health and life-skills support to help them stay employed.

Those programs will help decide whether East New Yorkers are the ones earning union wages and benefits on library renovations, school upgrades, and future housing developments, or whether they remain spectators to construction sites in their own backyard.

Nurse’s careful nod to past service, her decision not to name the outgoing Assembly member, and her public embrace of Christian Celeste Tate all came in the same moment she was laying out a hard line on vouchers and NYCHA repairs. With Nurse pushing from City Hall, Lucas moving money and programs through the state budget, and Tate preparing to take office, CB5 now sits at the center of a potential power alignment around one core question: can this community turn budget lines and legal programs into real keys, real deeds, and real paychecks for the people who already live here.
